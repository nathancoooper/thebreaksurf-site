Organic cotton gets thrown around a lot as a buzzword, so we wanted to actually explain what it means, and why it's one of the non-negotiables at The Break.

At its simplest, organic cotton is grown without synthetic pesticides, fertilisers, or genetically modified seeds. That sounds like a small distinction, but it isn't. Conventional cotton is one of the most chemically intensive crops in the world; it uses a disproportionate share of global pesticides for the amount of farmland it occupies. Organic farming methods, by contrast, rely on crop rotation and natural processes to keep the soil healthy, which in turn uses less water and keeps the surrounding ecosystem, and the farmers working it, safer.

But "organic" on its own isn't a guarantee of anything unless it's backed up by proper certification. That's why we source our cotton through Stanley/Stella, who we chose specifically because they don't just claim to be sustainable, they prove it.

Stanley/Stella's cotton is GOTS certified (Global Organic Textile Standard), which is the industry's gold standard. It doesn't just look at the raw cotton, it tracks the entire supply chain, from the farm through to the finished garment, and includes both ecological and social criteria along the way. On top of that, their products carry OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certification, confirming there are no harmful chemicals in the finished fabric, and Global Recycled Standard (GRS) certification for the recycled materials they use elsewhere in their range. They're also a member of the Fair Wear Foundation, which focuses on the people behind the product, not just the cotton itself, and their materials are PETA-Approved Vegan.

We could have gone with a cheaper, uncertified "organic" cotton and taken the label at face value. We didn't want to do that. Part of being genuinely sustainable is being willing to have your supply chain checked, not just described.

None of this means organic cotton is a perfect fix. It still needs water, and it's not a magic solution to the fashion industry's environmental footprint. But it's a meaningfully better starting point than conventional cotton, and paired with the way we treat everything else, from packaging to postage, it's one more piece of doing this properly rather than just doing it quickly.

If you've got questions about where any of our materials come from, ask us. We'd rather explain it properly than let a label do the talking.
